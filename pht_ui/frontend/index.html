PHT Literature Review

Upload a research paper and see how four AI models code it against the PHT framework.

Why this project? Click to expand ∨

The problem. Coding 215 accessibility research papers against an eight-dimension framework by hand is slow work. Two human reviewers coded each paper and reached consensus through discussion, but this process took the team roughly two years. We want to know whether large language models can produce reliable framework codings faster, and where they agree or disagree with trained human coders.

The framework. PHT (Playful Health Technology) is a framework from Duval (2022) that characterizes games and play systems in accessibility research across eight design dimensions — such as whether the system is fun-first or utility-first, solo or social, competitive or collaborative. Each dimension uses a five-point scale. This interface shows a paper's score on each dimension, as coded by four different language models and (where available) by the human consensus.

Why these four models. Each of the four models represents a distinct choice in the AI landscape, which lets us see whether agreement across models is a reliable signal of correctness:

  • GPT-5.1 (OpenAI) — a commercial closed-source flagship, representing the state of the art in proprietary models.
  • Gemini 2.5 Flash (Google) — a commercial closed model optimized for speed; included to test whether a fast, cheaper model can match the flagship.
  • Llama 3.3 70B (Meta, served by Groq) — a large open-weight model running on specialized inference hardware. Represents the open-source tier at scale.
  • Mistral Small (Mistral AI) — a European open-weight model from a different lab; different training data and architecture choices than Llama.

How the models arrive at an answer. The system reads the PDF, splits it into passages, searches for passages relevant to the dimension being coded, and sends those passages to each model along with a structured prompt. The prompt asks the model to explain its reasoning before naming an answer, to cite the specific passages it used, and to rate its own confidence. When a paper does not describe a game or play system, or when the evidence is insufficient, the model can say so honestly rather than guess.

How to read the heatmap. Each row is one PHT dimension, each column is one model. A cell's color shows the answer (1–5 on the viridis scale). The small tag in the corner tells you how the model arrived at the answer — whether it read the full paper, retrieved specific passages, or had insufficient evidence. When human consensus data is available for a paper, it appears as a fifth column with a green outline; model cells that match the human answer get a green border, and cells that differ get a red border.
